# Breathing phase, RT and multisensory synchrony

Targeted literature check, 23 October 2026. **The current raw RT direction is
consistent with some published results, but does not establish a direct
replication or a universal advantage of exhalation.** Tasks and respiratory
measurements differ, and contrary findings must remain visible.

## What this dataset currently shows

In the exploratory primary40 analysis, the estimated Inhale−Exhale differences
are positive for AT 300/800/1500/2200/2700 ms and pooled baseline: +16.41,
+8.94, +29.90, +22.91, +14.18 and +20.63 ms. Several pointwise intervals include
zero. These six estimates do not test whether the phase effect differs between
SOAs. The overall raw AT contrast is +18.47 ms; after matched baseline subtraction,
it is −2.16 ms, 95% CI [−12.15, 7.82], inconclusive. See the
[complete results, methods and figure](../results/phase_rt/RESULTS.md).

The labels are scheduled/instructed phases in the released CSV. The available
release cannot validate actual breathing, precise respiratory angle, or phase
at stimulus versus response. This limits comparison with continuously monitored
respiration. A general RT phase difference is also not a PPS boundary effect.

## Closest same-direction evidence

**Buchsbaum and Callaway (1965), _Influences of Respiratory Cycle on Simple
Reaction Time_.** The publisher abstract reports faster simple auditory responses
during expiration in two experiments involving spontaneous respiration and
breath-holding conditions. This agrees with the sign of our raw RT contrast;
the modality, respiratory manipulation and estimand differ. Only the abstract
was accessible. [Publisher record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pms.1965.20.3.961).

**Yamazaki and Paller (2026), _Response speed is modulated by respiratory phase_.**
Northwestern's institutional report describes 35 adults performing a visual
psychomotor-vigilance task with measured nasal airflow, reporting responses about
41 ms faster during exhalation than inhalation. This is a useful same-direction
comparison for a simple speeded response. The journal article was inaccessible
to this search, so details and magnitude remain based on that institutional
report, not an independent full-paper audit.
[Institutional report](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2026/09/ready-set-exhale-study-links-respiration-and-cognition),
[paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2026.117535).

## Relevant multisensory and tactile evidence

**Saltafossi et al. (2025), _Respiration Facilitates Behavior During Multisensory
Integration_.** Forty participants received unimodal and bimodal stimuli,
including audio–tactile stimuli, with belt-measured respiration. RT was faster
around peak inspiration/early expiration and slower near the expiration-to-
inspiration transition. This supports fine-grained respiratory modulation, but
does not predict a simple whole-inhale versus whole-exhale difference. Its race-
model integration measure also differs from our baseline subtraction or PPS fit.
[Full paper](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.70145).

**Grund et al. (2022), _Respiration, Heartbeat, and Conscious Tactile Perception_.**
In 41 participants, near-threshold tactile detection was highest just after
expiration onset, with respiratory/cardiac activity recorded. This is relevant
somatosensory context, but detection rate is a different outcome from RT and
cannot count as a replication of our latency contrast.
[Primary abstract and figures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34853084/).

## Contrary findings and timing limits

**Molle et al. (2023), _Inhalation boosts perceptual awareness and decision
speed_.** A 30-person near-threshold visual orientation task found faster
objective decisions during inhalation, the opposite direction to our raw RT
estimate. It argues against treating either phase as universally faster.
[Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37529836/).

**Johannknecht and Kayser (2022), _The influence of the respiratory cycle on
reaction times in sensory-cognitive paradigms_.** Across six tasks, respiration
related to RT, with results depending on the task and whether respiration was
aligned to stimuli or responses. This supports examining timing and task
differences rather than assuming that every binary phase comparison estimates
the same effect. [Full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06364-8).

**Harting et al. (2025), _Respiration shapes response speed and accuracy with a
systematic time lag_.** Across 12 datasets, the strongest RT association involved
respiration roughly two seconds before the response. Such lagged, continuous-
phase results cannot be recovered from our released binary labels.
[Primary abstract and figures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40199358/).

## Multisensory synchrony-detection tasks

The owner clarified that the relevant tasks judge synchrony **between external
sensory stimuli** (audio, touch or vision), rather than synchrony with a heartbeat
or breath. In the targeted search, no directly matching study was identified
that compared inhale versus exhale in an external audio–tactile or audio–visual
simultaneity-judgment task. This is a search limitation, not evidence that no such
study exists. Saltafossi et al. is the closest respiratory/multisensory match
above, but used speeded detection and a race model, not synchrony judgments.

**Leone and McCourt (2015), _Dissociation of perception and action in audiovisual
multisensory integration_.** This is the most useful comparison for interpreting
our RT result. Their RT/race-model experiment and explicit simultaneity/order
judgments had different timing requirements: changing the visual stimulus
condition shifted perceived simultaneity, whereas race-model facilitation was
restricted to physical simultaneity. The judgment experiment had six observers.
This directly cautions against using an RT benefit as a proxy for perceived
synchrony. It did not test breathing phase.
[Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715611/).

**Biau et al. (2022), _Left Motor δ Oscillations Reflect Asynchrony Detection in
Multisensory Speech Perception_.** Twenty-three analyzed participants judged
whether speech videos and audio were synchronous or delayed by 400 ms. Masking
the face reduced asynchrony sensitivity and slowed responses. However, the main
effect of asynchrony on RT was inconclusive (p=.42), illustrating that synchrony
sensitivity and response latency need separate measurements. The task had no
response deadline; it supplies no inhale/exhale comparison.
[Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8936602/).

**Han et al. (2022), _The development of audio–visual temporal precision precedes
its rapid recalibration_.** Reanalysis of audio–visual, audio–tactile and
visual–tactile simultaneity judgments found that rapid recalibration differed
across modality pairs. This provides an audio–tactile task reference and warns
against automatically transferring an audio–visual finding to touch. It studied
development and trial-history effects, not respiration.
[Full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-25392-y).

**Zamfira et al. (online 2025; issue 2026), _The rhythm of sensory input shapes
audio-visual temporal processing_.** Simultaneity judgments for pulsing audiovisual
streams showed narrower temporal binding windows at higher stimulation
frequencies and wider windows for speech-like streams. This concerns external
stimulus rhythm, not the breathing cycle. The primary abstract and figure
descriptions were accessible; full text was blocked during this search.
[Primary record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40956654/).

**Clouston, Davidson and Alais (online 2025; issue 2026), _Perception of audio-visual
synchrony is modulated by walking speed and step-cycle phase_.** Twenty-one
participants made audiovisual synchrony judgments while walking. Slow walking
widened the synchrony window and increased RT; judgments also varied with
step-cycle phase. This is a useful example of a bodily rhythm modulating explicit
synchrony perception, but walking phase cannot predict an inhale/exhale effect.
[Full paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03010066251398424).

**Implication for this dataset:** the six phase deltas answer how quickly people
responded to the tactile target. They do not estimate the point of subjective
simultaneity, temporal binding-window width or synchrony-discrimination
sensitivity. A test of that hypothesis would need explicit synchrony responses
across sensory timing offsets, alongside measured respiratory phase. The current
RT result cannot be called a confirmation of a synchrony-detection result.

## Wording justified by this comparison

The raw response-level pattern is **directionally consistent with some reports
of faster responding during expiration**. It is not a direct replication, proof
of causality, a universal respiratory law, or evidence of a respiratory effect
on PPS boundaries. The similar baseline change and inconclusive corrected mean
contrast limit an interpretation specific to audio–tactile facilitation.

## Search and access record

This was a targeted comparison, not a systematic review or meta-analysis.
Matching simple-RT studies, multisensory/tactile studies, timing dependence and
opposite-direction results were searched. A focused follow-up covered external
multisensory simultaneity, temporal-order and asynchrony-detection tasks, with a
countercheck for RT–judgment dissociations and non-respiratory rhythms.
Consensus required reauthentication;
native web search and opened publisher, PubMed and institutional pages were used.
The source/access ledger records what was actually available, including blocked
full texts: [queries and source records](respiration_rt_sources.json). Published
effect sizes were not standardized or statistically compared with this dataset.
