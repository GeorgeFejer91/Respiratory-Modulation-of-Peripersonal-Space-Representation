Respiratory Modulation of Peripersonal Space Representation

George Fejer1,2,3, Till Holzapfel4, Michael Gaebler2, and Bigna Lenggenhager5,6

The authors acknowledge support from the BIAL Foundation for the Viscereality research programme (grant 308, 2022 cycle).

Abstract

We tested whether breath-coupled virtual reality alters the relationship between respiratory phase and tactile reaction time (RT) during an approaching sound. Participants performed guided box breathing in Viscereality and Control, and responded to chest vibrations at five sound-to-touch intervals. The behavioural analysis used 40 of 42 released records after excluding two records with unresolved linkage. The planned condition-by-phase-by-interval interaction in raw RT was inconclusive (p = .873 in the 40-record sensitivity). Raw audio–tactile responses were 18.47 ms slower after inhalation than after exhalation, while the difference after matched baseline subtraction was −2.16 ms (95% CI [−12.15, 7.82] ms). Individual sigmoid midpoints and sharpness were weakly identified. In an exploratory comparison of 41 paired self-reports, 22 spatial-frame ratings were higher after Viscereality, 13 were unchanged, and 6 were lower (two-sided exact sign test, p = .00372). The results distinguish a shift in reported spatial experience from uncertain effects on behavioural measures of peripersonal space.

Keywords · peripersonal space, respiration, virtual reality, interoception, multisensory integration

Introduction

Peripersonal space (PPS) is the region near the body where nearby events can alter responses to touch. In an audio–tactile task, a sound approaches the body and a touch arrives at different delays. Shorter tactile reaction times at some points on the trajectory have been used to infer the spatial extent of multisensory facilitation (Canzoneri et al., 2012). As the sound approaches, elapsed time and simulated distance change together. The task therefore requires evidence that separates a spatial interpretation from response preparation and temporal expectancy.

Breathing provides both an internal sensation and a signal that can drive visual feedback. Viscereality renders the breath as expansion and contraction of the surrounding virtual scene (Fejer et al., 2026). The study asked whether the audio–tactile response across stimulus onset asynchronies (SOAs) differed between post-inhalation and post-exhalation holds, and whether breath-coupled visuals amplified that difference relative to Control (Fejer et al., 2025). We also examined whether the intervention changed the self-reported spatial frame of reference and whether baseline interoceptive sensibility was associated with that change.

Methods

Design and task

Participants completed both conditions in a single sitting with counterbalanced order. Viscereality coupled the spatial expansion and contraction of the virtual scene to breathing. Control presented a performance indicator on a dark field. In each condition participants followed a 4–4–4–4 s breathing cycle, with trials scheduled during the holds after inhalation or exhalation.

Each full session comprised 12 blocks of 34 trials. Audio–tactile trials presented a looming binaural sound and a chest vibration at one of five SOAs (300, 800, 1,500, 2,200, or 2,700 ms). Baseline trials presented the vibration alone, and catch trials presented sound alone. Participants clicked a mouse as quickly as possible after detecting vibration and withheld responses on catch trials. The released data contain scheduled phase labels. Respiratory belt recordings were unavailable for the analysis.

Records and preprocessing

The deidentified release contains 42 participant records (34 recorded as female and 8 as male, aged 19–37 years, M = 23.00, SD = 3.98) and 17,114 trial rows. Two records share a behavioural recording with reversed condition labels and different questionnaire responses. The current behavioural analyses exclude both from the primary cohort (40 records, 8,478 retained audio–tactile RTs), with inclusion of either or both examined in sensitivity analyses. Questionnaire-only analyses use the available paired ratings independently of behavioural linkage. The investigator confirmed ethics approval in the data-release statement.

Eligible audio–tactile and tactile-only hits with an observed RT were pooled within person to calculate the mean and sample SD. The fixed trim retained values within 2.5 SD of that mean. The analysis plan also excludes participants with catch false-alarm rates above 30%. A 200 ms lower cutoff was examined as a sensitivity. For corrected RT we subtracted each participant’s retained tactile-only mean for the same condition and scheduled phase from each audio–tactile RT. The resulting negative values indicate faster audio–tactile responses relative to that baseline. The preprocessing companion traces these rules through the data.

Outcomes and analysis

The planned test compared trial-level Gaussian mixed models of raw audio–tactile RT, with a participant random intercept and condition, scheduled phase, and numeric SOA as predictors. A likelihood-ratio test evaluated the three-way term. This model estimates a difference in SOA slopes. Curve analyses separately assessed whether the data identified an individual sigmoid midpoint and normalized midpoint sharpness, including fit diagnostics, sensitivity to shape assumptions, and predictive checks.

The 37-item Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness, Version 2 (MAIA-2) was completed once before the experiment (Mehling et al., 2018). Participants selected a pictographic Spatial Frame of Reference Continuum category after each condition (Fejer et al., 2026). An exploratory paired sign test used category ordering. An exploratory Spearman analysis related baseline MAIA-2 scores to numerical condition differences, which assumes comparable steps between adjacent categories. The full analysis specification documents the models, exclusions, and later analysis amendments. The AsPredicted record describes the timing of registration relative to data collection.

Results

Do general reaction times differ between inhalation and exhalation?

Respiratory phase may influence response speed independently of audio–tactile facilitation. We therefore compare raw RTs in each trial type and ask whether the phase difference remains after subtracting each participant’s condition- and phase-matched baseline.

Raw audio–tactile responses were 18.47 ms slower during inhale than exhale (95% CI [6.96, 29.98] ms; paired p = .0024): mean RTs were 680.66 and 662.19 ms, respectively. Baseline RT also increased during inhale, by 20.63 ms. After matched-baseline subtraction, the corrected phase effect was inconclusive: Inhale − Exhale = −2.16 ms, 95% CI [−12.15, 7.82] ms, p = .664. Corrected means were −19.73 ms during inhale and −17.57 ms during exhale. This pattern is compatible with a general response-speed difference; it does not establish an additional phase effect specific to audio–tactile facilitation or that inhalation alone caused the difference.

Evidence from related studies

Published findings support respiratory modulation of response speed, with task-dependent direction and timing. Buchsbaum and Callaway (1965) reported faster simple auditory responses during expiration (publisher abstract). In contrast, Molle et al. (2023) found faster objective orientation decisions during inhalation. Across six sensory–cognitive tasks, Johannknecht and Kayser (2022) found RT associations in several tasks, strongest when respiration was aligned to the response rather than stimulus onset. These differences argue against a universal advantage of either phase.

The closest multisensory comparison is Saltafossi et al. (2025). In 40 participants receiving auditory, visual and tactile stimuli alone or in pairs, responses were faster around peak inspiration and early expiration and slower around the expiration-to-inspiration transition. Yet race-model measures of audio–tactile and audio–visual integration were greatest around that transition. Thus, the respiratory timing of faster responses need not coincide with stronger multisensory integration. This was a speeded detection task, not a judgment of stimulus synchrony.

Our raw RT direction agrees with some expiration-advantage reports, but the similar baseline change and inconclusive corrected contrast limit a multisensory-specific interpretation. The released phases are scheduled or instructed labels; unavailable belt recordings prevent physiological validation. These RTs do not measure subjective simultaneity or a temporal binding window. This is a targeted literature overview, not a systematic review; the extended comparison and access record retain further studies and limitations.

Did baseline-corrected RTs decline across blocks?

There was no clear systematic RT decline across the 12 chronological blocks spanning both consecutive conditions. For AT − matched Baseline RT, the average participant slope was −0.12 ms per block (95% CI −1.32 to 1.08; t(38) = −0.20, p = .840).

Do the RT observations support a sigmoid response?

No participant-level trial fit met the planned R² ≥ .70 criterion in the 168 provisional cells. Shape-constrained group curves produced visible transitions, while held-distance prediction from the Bayesian sigmoid was only 0.59 ms better than a constant (95% CI [−2.45, 1.13] ms for the RMSE difference).

Analysis and evidence

Can midpoint and sharpness be estimated reliably?

None of the 160 midpoint intervals in the 40-record cohort fell wholly inside the measured 10–100 cm range under the primary Bayesian prior. Within-session split-half rank correlations for the condition change were .032 for midpoint and −.134 for sharpness.

Analysis and evidence

Do condition, breathing phase or their interaction change PPS?

Estimated differences in midpoint and sharpness remained uncertain across the four condition-by-phase cells. The original fit gate left the planned boundary analysis unestimated. The exploratory Bayesian contrasts use posterior uncertainty and are conditional on the model.

Analysis and evidence

Are the results robust to analysis assumptions?

The analyses examined recording exclusions, alternative shape assumptions, preprocessing rules, and chronological trial position. The sensitivity analyses did not establish a reliable condition or phase difference in either curve parameter.

Analysis and evidence

How do the four average response curves differ?

Equal-participant average response functions were shallow across Control and Viscereality at both scheduled phases. Conditional midpoint averages clustered near the 55 cm prior centre, consistent with the weak information in the individual curves.

Discussion

The predicted Viscereality-specific modulation of PPS by respiratory phase remained inconclusive. Raw tactile responses were slower after inhalation in both audio–tactile and tactile-only trials. Subtracting the condition- and phase-matched baseline left a small, imprecise phase contrast. This pattern is consistent with a broader association between scheduled phase and response speed. Prior tasks have found phase associations whose timing and direction vary with the outcome (Johannknecht & Kayser, 2022), including differences between response speed and multisensory integration (Saltafossi et al., 2025).

Several measurement limits constrain interpretation. The tactile cue occurred about 500 ms later in audio–tactile trials than in baseline trials with the same nominal delay in audited recordings. The offset was verified in recordings from 15 participants and the remaining recordings lack comparable timing verification. The regular breathing schedule and looming trajectory also couple time to simulated distance. Weakly identified sigmoid parameters and unavailable respiratory waveforms limit claims about a physiological PPS boundary.

Participants more often chose a spatially extended frame of reference after Viscereality than after Control. This paired ordinal result concerns reported experience. The association with baseline MAIA-2 Trusting was exploratory, used numerical category steps, and was unadjusted for multiple comparisons. The behavioural and self-report results support keeping reported spatial experience and task-derived PPS estimates distinct.
