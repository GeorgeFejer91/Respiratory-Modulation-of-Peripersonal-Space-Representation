Why examine sigmoid PPS curves? A miniature literature review

Evidence review: 16 October 2026 (Europe/Berlin). This focused narrative review explains the rationale and limits of the Study 5 exploratory analysis. It is not a systematic review, a preregistration, or evidence that the desired curve must exist.

The defensible aim is to assess whether a common, transparent analysis can describe four distance-dependent reaction-time profiles with identifiable transitions. A sigmoid is a theory-motivated candidate. Smoothing and shape constraints are tools whose influence must be measured; they cannot establish a physiological boundary merely by producing a smooth S.

1. The theoretical case for a sigmoid is plausible, not compulsory

In the looming-sound paradigm, sound distance is related to tactile reaction time. Canzoneri, Magosso and Serino (2012) provide direct audiotactile precedent for investigating a distance-dependent transition. Noel et al. (2020) report sigmoid visuotactile facilitation and rapid recalibration with stimulus history. These studies motivate asking about transition location and sharpness. Their accessible abstracts do not validate Study 5's particular bandwidths, trimming percentages, parameter bounds, or respiratory manipulation.

A logistic curve provides interpretable descriptive quantities:

RT(d) = a + A / (1 + exp(-(d-m)/s)).

Here m is the half-amplitude location, s is a positive distance scale, and the midpoint RT slope is A/(4s). The 10–90% transition spans 2 ln(9) s. Scale s is not a Gaussian standard deviation, and a fitted midpoint is not automatically an independently measured PPS boundary. For A=0, location and scale are unidentified. With only five positions, good curve prediction can coexist with poorly determined parameters. The broader distinction between fit and parameter identifiability is supported by Raue et al. (2009), whose work concerns dynamical models rather than this RT model.

PPS theory also permits alternatives. The inspected author code associated with Straka, Noel and Hoffmann (2022) computes transition and slope summaries from impact-prediction profiles. This is mechanistic model output, not an empirical validation of a logistic fit to human RTs. It supports considering location and slope as descriptive questions without assuming that one response function is mandatory.

2. Contrary evidence is central to the rationale

Hobeika et al. (2020) explicitly question mandatory sigmoid fitting. Their abstract describes a fixed-distance control for expectancy and a distance effect that becomes linear on a logarithmic distance scale after accounting for expectancy. This matters because apparent distance effects can depend on stimulus propagation and anticipation, as well as spatial processing. Study 5's nominal Baseline delay labels do not establish the same control: trial-relative tactile-cue timing cannot be assumed equal across trial types. Smoothing cannot repair that design limitation.

Holmes et al. (2020), reporting four experiments and a meta-analysis, describe mixed support, small effects and concerns about expectancy, controls and flexible analyses. This is a particularly important counterweight to selecting only papers that report clear boundaries. The source set therefore supports investigating distance-related responses, but does not establish agreement that all such responses should be sigmoid. A claim of field-wide consensus would exceed this limited review.

The three most directly useful starting papers are Canzoneri for experimental precedent, Hobeika for a specific alternative explanation and response form, and Holmes for critical experimental and meta-analytic evidence. Noel adds evidence from a related sensory paradigm. Differences in modality, body location, expectancy controls and outcome definitions limit direct transfer to Study 5.

3. Why examine robust summaries and smoothing?

RT summaries can be sensitive to extreme responses and exclusion rules. Ratcliff (1993) compares approaches to RT outliers and reports that robust distribution summaries can be less affected by outliers and cutoffs than moment-based measures. This motivates checking means, medians, trimmed means and winsorized means. It does not establish that a specific 20% rule is optimal here, that slow responses are invalid, or that these summaries estimate the same quantity. Study 5 retains the original RT analysis and treats these additional summaries as sensitivity choices.

Local-constant and local-linear kernels, local weighted regression, curvature penalties and isotonic regression are established estimation families. The evidence table links the inspected official implementations and their methodological references. A smoother trades variability for bias; an isotonic fit imposes monotonicity. Neither creates new measurements. The Study 5 discrete penalty is not an exact smoothing spline, and its tricube candidate is not the full iteratively robust LOWESS algorithm. These distinctions prevent borrowing a method's reputation for a different implementation.

Trying several methods is defensible when the purpose is to reveal dependence on choices. Steegen et al. (2016) motivate reporting a range of reasonable processing choices because a single selected analysis can conceal fragile conclusions. Study 5's finite grid is inspired by that transparency principle. It is not an exhaustive multiverse: choices about the grid, cleaning, coordinates and curve families remain outside it. Reporting only the smoothest or most sigmoid-looking winner would defeat this rationale.

4. What makes the comparison informative?

Every condition uses the same candidate set, selection rule and bounds. Each person receives equal weight after within-person extraction. Candidates predict the same centered, unsmoothed mean-RT contrast in held-out people, so they are not rewarded simply for fitting their own smoothed targets. Equally processed linear fits and a zero-contrast constant provide simpler benchmarks.

Varma and Simon (2006) show that reporting the minimum cross-validation error used to select a model can be optimistic; their simulations support nesting tuning inside an outer evaluation. The principle motivates Study 5's inner pipeline selection and outer participant folds, although their classifier experiments are not a validation of this particular regression design. Varoquaux et al. (2017) further caution that cross-validation itself can be variable in small scientific datasets. Tiny differences between RMSEs should not be treated as decisive tests.

The evaluation conditions on the already-cleaned participant profiles. Initial participant-wide RT thresholds use all that person's eligible trials, including the position later omitted from fitting. Training and test people remain separate, but this is not an end-to-end prospective prediction of an unseen position from uncleaned trials. The common scoring target also measures relative profile shape, not absolute RT accuracy. Method families and visual thresholds were developed after observing the data, so nesting does not retroactively make them confirmatory.

Participant-cluster bootstrapping preserves the four repeated-measure profiles together. Its present intervals condition on the selected pipeline and imposed bounds; parameter intervals additionally exclude flat fits. This is useful conditional uncertainty, not a complete account of model-search uncertainty.

5. What the present data justify

The verified browser report contains 42 participants and five nominal positions. None of the original 320 pipelines met the specified visible-transition criteria in all four curves. The selected original curves had 10–90% spans of approximately 134–187 cm, exceeding the sampled 90-cm range. A shape-constrained search produced four visible S curves, but three scales reached the imposed floor and one midpoint reached the sampling limit.

Nested prediction RMSE was 47.634 ms for the constrained family, 47.701 ms for the tuned linear family, 47.772 ms for the original sigmoid family, and 47.437 ms for the constant. These are descriptive scores, not independent replicate tests. The unsmoothed curvature tests did not establish nonlinearity after correction; that also does not establish linearity. The participant-level boundary gate remains 0/168. A group curve cannot supply the missing participant boundaries.

The supported conclusion is therefore an exploratory, reproducible analysis of how extraction and shape assumptions affect four group profiles. The data do not presently establish four identified physiological PPS boundaries. Further work should prioritize the unresolved checks in the rationale document and an independent design with better transition sampling and expectancy control. Adding more smoothers solely until the curves look persuasive would not strengthen this conclusion.

Access and interpretation limits

Consensus MCP was unavailable; it was not used. The installed multi-source search skill guided PubMed discovery, exact-title refinement, and official/author-source inspection. The nine empirical or methodological papers used here were inspected at indexed-abstract level; six method references were checked through official software sources, and the PPS impact model through author code. Full-paper retrieval attempts were blocked or returned browser challenges. Current-literature queries also encountered challenges. This review consequently supports bounded claims from the inspected material, not detailed replications of inaccessible papers or an exhaustive account of the literature through 2026.
